# Design

The lab reproduces an employee lifecycle across Google Workspace, Apple MDM, and ChromeOS on a handful of devices. This document holds the design, the decisions behind it, and what would change at a few hundred users. Per-platform settings are in `workspace/tenant-settings.md`, `mdm/blueprints.md`, and `chromeos/policies.md`; procedures are in `runbooks/`; the reasoning behind each larger choice is in `decisions/`.

## Lifecycle flow

Three events, one command each, driven by a role catalog (`lifecycle/`). The role is written into the directory as the account's department; nothing else is stored, so every command reads the account and converges it to the catalog, a second run changes nothing, and an audit can run at any time.

**Join.** The hire record arrives: a ticket or an HRIS event in a company, a row in a CSV here. The account is created in the role's organizational unit, never in the root, with the username convention, a random first password, and the role's title, department, and manager. The role's groups grant access: the all-staff list, the department list, and for engineers the SAML grant to the Fleet console, where the account appears by itself at the first sign-in. The welcome kit, rendered from the role's template, tells the person about the first sign-in, the one-day 2-Step Verification window and the allowed methods, their personal phone, and their device. Then the device: a Mac already provisioned into Fleet is mapped to the person and added to the role label, which makes the role's apps arrive through install policies; a Chromebook is annotated with the person and moved to the device OU; a contractor gets nothing, because the controls for someone's own computer are at the account layer. The run ends with a read-back and the checklist of what a human still does: send the kit, issue the password, hand over the device, confirm the second factor after the first sign-in.

**Change.** A transfer or reorg moves the OU if the new role's differs, adds the groups the new role grants, removes the groups other catalog roles grant, keeps and reports any grant no role makes, updates title and department, and moves the Mac between role labels or reclaims it when the new role entitles a different class of device. The exception rule is deliberate: the catalog is a role model, not a complete access model, and a transfer that silently strips a one-off grant creates the ticket it was meant to prevent; the audit lists exceptions for review and a strict option removes them where a policy wants a clean start.

**Leave.** Access is cut first while every byte of data stays where it is: app passwords, backup codes, and tokens revoked, sessions signed out, the account suspended, which also ends SAML sign-in everywhere. Drive and the calendars go to the manager through the Data Transfer API; mail is retained in the suspended mailbox and in Vault, and new mail is copied to the manager by a routing rule, the one step of the full form done in the console. Every group comes off; the account moves to the leavers' OU with the date and the earliest deletion date in its note. The work account is wiped from any personal device under Workspace management. The Mac is locked and returned to IT custody; the Chromebook is disabled; a Jamf Now device is a console step, since Jamf Now has no API. The console account that just-in-time provisioning created is deleted, because identity creates it and never removes it. Deletion comes last, thirty days on, and the delete script refuses until the earlier steps are done. The emergency form stops after the first step.

## Roles, access, and devices

**OUs carry policy, groups carry access, the catalog is data.** Settings inherit down the OU tree (session length, service controls, device policy), so the OU is where policy lives; a group is what grants something (a list, an app, a share), so groups are where access lives. There is no OU per department, because no policy in this tenant differs by department; the Active Directory habit of one OU per team is deferred until a policy needs it. The catalog is one small file per role, read as data by the scripts and by a reviewer: Engineer and Marketing on `/Staff` with a Mac, Sales on `/Staff` with a Chromebook, Contractor on `/Contractors` with no company device. A new leavers' OU, `/Offboarded`, is the Disabled Users OU: reports exclude it, a mistaken reactivation grants nothing, and the delete script checks it.

**One company device per person, chosen by the role; nobody gets two.** The rule is where the work happens: a role that lives in the browser gets a Chromebook, which is cheaper, updates itself, boots verified, and resets to clean with a powerwash, so a returned device is reissued in minutes; a role that needs local tooling gets a Mac; external people bring their own computer. Phones are personal on both platforms and managed by Google through the account the person adds, the Device Policy app on iOS and a work profile on Android, with an account wipe on exit that leaves personal data alone; the Apple MDM never sees a personal device, since Jamf Now's enrollment is full-device management and Apple's BYOD mode needs Managed Apple Accounts the lab does not have. The lab's iOS management sits at Basic only because the iPad is in Jamf Now and iOS allows one MDM; the planned move of the iPad to Google management as a user-owned device turns that setting on for the staff and contractor OUs, never the root, so the administrator's devices are never forced to enroll.

**Device trees and user trees.** ChromeOS device policy follows the device's OU, which is the `/Devices` tree; user policy follows the OU of whoever signs in. A Chromebook in `/Devices` used by a Sales hire in `/Staff` gets the device policy of the first and the user policy of the second, visible together at `chrome://policy`. The same shape exists in Fleet, where the Workstations fleet is the device baseline and the role label is the entitlement, and in Jamf Now, where the admin-workstation and employee-baseline blueprints are the two device classes. The stricter alternative for contractors, a company Chromebook in a locked-down device OU (sign-in restricted to the domain, no guest mode, no external storage, forced re-enrollment so a reset cannot escape management), is described and not built, as are shared devices with no owner and a loaner pool for first-day or repair cover.

**The contractor's computer is not enrolled anywhere.** The company has no business putting a management profile on a device it does not own, and often the device is already managed by the contractor's agency. What it controls is the account and the browser: the `/Contractors` OU's twelve-hour session and Drive sharing rules, user-level Chrome policy that follows the work account into whichever Chrome profile it signs into, and a suspension that revokes every token. Context-Aware Access adds the device signal at scale: Drive reachable from any device, download and sync only from an endpoint-verified, encrypted one.

**In Fleet, a role is a label whose definition lives in git and whose membership lives in the API.** The label is declared in `fleet/gitops/labels/` as a manual label with no host list; Fleet preserves a manual label's membership when the key is absent and clears it when the key is present, so an apply never touches who is in it, and a label created outside git is deleted on the next apply. Software and policies are scoped to the label in git (Slack for every Mac, VS Code for engineers, Zoom for marketing, each installed by a policy automation when missing); which Macs hold the label is set by the lifecycle scripts, because a device assignment is operational state, not configuration. The person-to-device link is Fleet's custom human-device mapping, set by the same scripts. The at-scale shape is Fleet's host-vitals label on the end user's department, fed by SCIM from the identity provider, which moves a device with its owner's department and needs no script at all; the Free-tier middle path is a hostname convention set at provisioning with a dynamic label on the prefix.

**Support patterns the automation removes.** Missing access on the first day, because a group was forgotten: the role grants it. Wrong OU, because the account was created in the root and moved later: created in place. A transfer that lost a one-off grant, or kept a department list the person left: the exception rule and the catalog rule. Application accounts outliving the person: the console account is deleted on exit, and the gap for applications without an API is written on the checklist. Devices that never came back: locked or disabled on exit, with the unlock on the checklist. Drift between what a role should have and what people have: `audit-access.sh`, run at any time, lists mismatches against the catalog and exceptions for review.

## Identity and access

**Context-Aware Access, intended policy.** Context-Aware Access is not available on the Business Plus edition this tenant runs, so the policy is designed here and not enforced. The intent: access to Gmail, Drive, and the Admin console is conditioned on device state as reported by endpoint verification or MDM, not only on who is signing in. Staff reach Gmail and Drive only from a device that is MDM-enrolled, encrypted, and on a current OS; contractors reach Drive from any device but cannot download or sync unless the device is managed; the Admin console is reachable only from a managed device. Each rule is an access level bound to an OU, which is why the OU structure separates staff from contractors: the policy attaches to the tree, not to people. What exists today instead is the combination of enforced 2-Step Verification, MDM enrollment, and a shorter session length for contractors. The same idea is built outside Google in a later phase with Cloudflare Access in front of the Fleet console, where a device-posture rule gates an internal application.

**Administrative tooling runs as the administrator, not as a service account.** GAM, the command-line tool behind the lifecycle scripts, is authorized through an OAuth client in a Cloud project the tenant owns, with the super admin's own consent and second factor. GAM could have created a service-account key; the lab runs without one, which Google's secure-by-default policy on a new Cloud organization also enforces. Every action in the scripts (accounts, groups, suspension, Drive ownership transfer) runs under the admin's authorization and is attributed to that admin in the audit log, and no long-lived key sits on the workstation. Domain-wide delegation, which lets a service account act as any user, is reserved for the day a task needs it, and then through keyless federation rather than a downloaded key. The cost showed up in the lifecycle work: GAM sends mail (the welcome kit, the initial-password notification, a leaver's auto-reply or delegation) only through a service account with delegation, acting as a user, so the welcome kit is rendered to a file the administrator sends from their own mailbox, the initial password is issued from the Admin console or handed over with the device, and a leaver's new mail reaches the manager through a Gmail routing rule set in the console, since routing has no API and a forwarding setting on the mailbox would need the delegation. Where delegation exists the same scripts send through GAM; at scale the mail comes from the HRIS or ticketing integration, or from a service identity scoped to sending and mailbox settings.

## Device management: two layers, one owner per platform

Every device that holds a Workspace account sits under Google's account layer: the device appears in the inventory, a screen lock can be required, and the account and its data can be wiped, all without a profile or an agent. That is Google's Basic mobile management, and it applies regardless of what else manages the device. The device layer, enrollment, configuration profiles, restrictions, encryption, apps, and a full wipe, has exactly one owner per device, and choosing that owner is the design decision. Setting Google's mobile management to Advanced for a platform makes Google that owner for it.

The lab splits by ownership class, which is how most Workspace shops split. Company-owned Apple hardware is managed by an Apple MDM: Jamf Now for the MacBook and the iPad, Fleet for the virtual machines. Google stays Basic on iOS so that it never competes with Jamf Now for the iPad, and Advanced on Android, where nothing else in the lab could own the platform. The production posture the lab does not build is the other half of the common shop: personal phones on both platforms under Google Advanced, a work profile on Android and Google's MDM profile on iOS through an Apple push certificate held in the Workspace console, with the Apple MDM reserved for company-owned hardware. A cross-platform product such as Workspace ONE or Intune is the alternative, one console for every ownership class and platform, with Google left at the account layer; it is the default in Microsoft-licensed environments and the exception in Google ones. An optional late step moves the iPad from Jamf Now to Google Advanced management as a user-owned device to show that migration; if it happens, the runbook and the inventory evidence say so.

**Where the Fleet server lives, and what that costs.** Fleet runs as a Docker Compose project on the admin workstation and is published at `fleet.tjcollins.dev` through a Cloudflare Tunnel: the connector opens an outbound connection to Cloudflare's edge, so the workstation exposes no inbound port and has no public address, and devices reach the server over HTTPS with a certificate Cloudflare issues and renews. TLS terminates at Cloudflare's edge and again at the tunnel; the only unencrypted hop is between the connector and the Fleet container on the Compose network inside the workstation. Cloudflare therefore sees the MDM and osquery traffic in the clear, a trusted middlebox in the same position as a load balancer doing TLS offload in front of an application server, which is also how Fleet's own reference deployments are built. The uptime dependency is explicit: the virtual machines check in only while the workstation is awake and the Compose project and tunnel are running. Nothing is lost while it is down, since fleetd queues its check-ins and MDM commands wait on the server for the next connection, but no policy result, profile, or command moves until it is back. That is acceptable for a lab whose managed Macs live on the same machine, and it is the first thing to change at scale.

**Configuration as code, and where the role split lives in Fleet.** Everything Fleet applies to a Mac is a file under `fleet/gitops/`, and `fleetctl gitops` makes the server match those files: a setting absent from them is reset to its default, a fleet absent from them is deleted, so the repository is the source of truth and every change is a commit that can be reviewed and reverted. The role split that Jamf Now expresses as two blueprints is expressed in Fleet as a fleet (Fleet's name for a team): the **Workstations** fleet carries the employee baseline, and the enroll secret baked into the fleetd package decides which fleet a Mac joins, the way an OU decides which policies a Windows machine receives. A second fleet for administrator workstations is where the MacBook's lighter baseline would live; it is not built, because the MacBook stays in Jamf Now. Inside the fleet, a `canary` label scopes a changed profile to one host first, then the label filter is removed and the profile reaches every host, which is the whole of the lab's rollout process. Fleet's own template pattern is a separate canary fleet with the same profile files listed in both fleet files, which reads better at scale, where a page per stage is clearer than a filter, at the cost of a second package and enroll secret; the two-machine lab uses the label, which also keeps working on Fleet Free. Fleets, enforced disk encryption, an enforced OS floor, and policy automations are Fleet Premium, used here on a trial; on Fleet Free the same profiles and policies sit in `default.yml` and apply to every host, and the canary label still works. Enroll secrets, the credential a new Mac presents to join, are declared in the YAML as variables and substituted at apply time from a file outside the repository; Fleet's newer default keeps them out of GitOps entirely and manages them in the console, which suits a pipeline that should not hold them, and the exception that enforces that default is turned off here so the repository stays the single source.

**Single sign-on to the console, and what just-in-time provisioning does and does not do.** The Fleet console is the lab's SAML service provider, with Google Workspace as the identity provider and access granted to one group; the setup is `runbooks/sso-app-setup.md`. A console account is created at the first sign-in (just-in-time provisioning, a Fleet Premium feature) as a read-only observer, so onboarding to the console is a group membership and nothing else. What JIT does not do is remove: suspending the Google account ends sign-in, and the offboarding runbook deletes the console account by hand. At scale that gap is closed by SCIM where the app supports it, or by an offboarding script that walks every SAML app's user list; roles above observer come from a directory attribute mapped into the SAML response rather than from hand edits in the app. One administrator keeps a password and stays out of SSO, so the console can be reached when the identity provider cannot.

## What changes at 500 users

(to be written; specifics gathered while building)

- The Fleet server moves off a workstation to a host that is always on, with TLS terminated on infrastructure the organization owns or under a data-processing agreement with the proxy provider, since MDM traffic carries profiles and enrollment secrets.
- The Fleet database gets a backup: it is the system of record for enrollment, and rebuilding it means re-enrolling every device even though the configuration itself is reproducible from GitOps.
- Local administrator credentials on managed Macs are rotated by the MDM rather than shared, and FileVault keys are escrowed on every device, not demonstrated on one.
- GitOps runs from a pipeline on merge, under an API-only user holding the GitOps role, rather than from an administrator's workstation with an administrator's token; a pull request is the change record and the dry run is its check.
- The trigger for the lifecycle becomes the HRIS event or the ticket transition, and the record of each run lives on the ticket; the scripts move from an administrator's workstation, where a person answers the prompts, to a service on an always-on runner under a service identity, and from bash around two command-line tools to Python against the Admin SDK and the Fleet API, with retries, structured errors, and tests.
- SCIM carries the role into the applications: console accounts in Fleet, Slack, and every other SAML app are created and removed by the identity provider rather than by just-in-time provisioning and an offboarding checklist, and Fleet's host vitals receive the end user's department, so the role label becomes a dynamic label and the device assignment stops being a step.
- A service identity scoped to sending and mailbox settings, or the integration platform, sends the welcome kit and the password and sets forwarding to the manager on a leaver's mailbox, since routing rules accumulate one per leaver and nothing can create them for you. Delegation, which opens the whole mailbox, stays an admin-approved, logged exception and never a default.
- Macs arrive through Automated Device Enrollment from Apple Business Manager, assigned to a fleet before they are unboxed, with the person's identity bound at setup; Chromebooks through zero-touch enrollment; the user-approved enrollment and the hand-built package become the exception path.
- Groups by directory attribute (dynamic groups, an Enterprise feature) replace the catalog's group list for the department lists, so a role change in the HRIS moves list membership without a script; the catalog keeps the entitlements that are decisions rather than attributes.
- Context-Aware Access gates the SaaS suite on device state, which turns the contractor's own-computer posture from "session length and sharing rules" into "no download without a verified device".
- Two super administrators, neither used for daily work, and the lifecycle scripts under a delegated admin role scoped to users, groups, and devices rather than the super admin.
